Bioprinting clinical applications in tissue engineering are starting to move beyond proof-of-concept demonstrations. A first bioprinted corneal implant is now being tested in a Phase 1 human trial. The vascularized, skin and cartilage constructs described in the research cited below were tested in the laboratory or in animals.

This article covers where the technology stands for a few tissue types, and why vascular network fabrication remains the central engineering problem for thicker implantable grafts.

---

## Why Vascularization Defines the Ceiling

Every printed tissue construct faces the same constraint: cells need oxygen. One review of vascular bioprinting puts the diffusion limit of oxygen at 100 to 200 micrometers, depending on the tissue, and notes that cell survival beyond that limit appears to be compromised. The same review says spontaneous vascularization might be sufficient for thin tissues such as skin or avascular ones such as cartilage, and that complications arise with larger defects. Without perfusable vessels, three-dimensional engineered tissues densely populated with cells quickly develop a necrotic core.

Vascularization is not simply a matter of printing hollow channels. Reviews of the field describe work toward branched, hierarchical networks, channels lined with endothelial cells, and integration with the host tissue once a construct is implanted.

Extrusion-based bioprinting can produce channels using sacrificial inks, which are printed and later washed out to leave hollow channels. Pluronic F-127 and carbohydrate glass are among the sacrificial inks used. Coaxial nozzle systems are another route: an inner nozzle extrudes a crosslinker while an outer nozzle extrudes the hydrogel, so the printed strand keeps a hollow cylindrical shape. According to one mini-review, these extrusion methods are usually constrained to channel diameters above 200 to 300 micrometers. Laser-based printing is listed in one review as time consuming.

A 2012 paper in Nature Materials used 3D printed carbohydrate glass lattices as sacrificial templates for perfusable vascular networks in engineered tissue. Endothelial cells seeded through a single inlet lined the walls of the network, the channels supported perfusion of human blood, and primary hepatocytes in gels with perfused channels showed higher albumin secretion and urea synthesis than those in gels without channels. That work was done in vitro, and the authors mention clinical implantation only as a future goal.

## Tissue Types and Where They Stand

### Skin

In-situ skin bioprinting, where a printer scans a wound and deposits cells directly onto it, has been demonstrated in animals. In a 2019 paper in Scientific Reports, researchers at the Wake Forest Institute for Regenerative Medicine described a mobile skin bioprinting system that uses integrated imaging to deliver dermal fibroblasts and epidermal keratinocytes into a wound in layers that mimic skin. In mouse wounds, printing autologous cells in a hydrogel carrier produced rapid wound closure, reduced contraction and accelerated re-epithelialization.

### Cartilage and Bone Scaffolds

Articular cartilage is avascular and has very limited capacity to regenerate itself.

A 2024 review of hydrogel-based bioprinting for articular cartilage covers bioinks based on gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA), alginate and decellularized extracellular matrix (dECM), seeded with chondrocytes, progenitor cells or mesenchymal stem cells. Mechanical properties remain a challenge: the review notes that alginate cannot match the mechanical requirements of native cartilage and that GelMA is hindered by low mechanical strength in load-bearing repair. One reinforcement strategy it describes is depositing layers of polycaprolactone (PCL) alongside an alginate hydrogel carrying chondrocytes.

For scaffolds that also address the underlying bone, the same review describes hydrogels combined with hydroxyapatite or beta-tricalcium phosphate. The studies it describes were carried out in animals, including rabbits, rats and mice.

### Corneal Tissue

Corneal tissue is where a bioprinted implant has reached patients. On October 29, 2025, a patient at Rambam Medical Center in Haifa, Israel received PB-001, an implant made by Precise Bio from corneal endothelial cells printed into a layered structure. The procedure was part of a Phase 1, single-arm trial in patients with corneal edema caused by endothelial dysfunction, with planned enrollment of 10 to 15 patients. The trial's primary focus is safety and tolerability, with early efficacy assessed at six months. By February 23, 2026 the company reported that three patients had been treated with no serious adverse event reported, and said it expected topline results in the fourth quarter of 2026.

This is an early safety trial of an implant for the endothelium, the cornea's inner cell layer. It is not an approved product.

## The Regulatory Landscape

In the United States, the FDA defines a combination product as one composed of any combination of a drug, a device and a biological product. A combination product is assigned to a lead center (CBER, CDER or CDRH) based on its primary mode of action, meaning the single mode of action that provides the most important therapeutic action. The type of marketing application follows from that: a biologics license application where the primary mode of action is that of a biological product, and a premarket approval application or 510(k) premarket notification where it is that of a device.

In Europe, medicines based on genes, tissues or cells are regulated as advanced therapy medicinal products (ATMPs). The category includes tissue-engineered medicines, which contain cells or tissues modified so they can be used to repair, regenerate or replace human tissue. An ATMP that includes a medical device as an integral part is called a combined ATMP; the European Medicines Agency gives cells embedded in a biodegradable matrix or scaffold as an example. All advanced therapy medicines are authorised centrally via the EMA, with its Committee for Advanced Therapies (CAT) playing a central role in the scientific assessment.

## What the Near Term Looks Like

The evidence so far follows the vascularization problem. The bioprinted implant that has reached a human trial is a thin layer of corneal endothelial cells. The skin and cartilage work described above was done in animals, and the perfusable vascular networks described above were demonstrated in vitro.

Bioprinting clinical applications in tissue engineering are not waiting for a single breakthrough. Evidence is accumulating construct by construct and trial by trial, and the first human safety data for a bioprinted implant are still being collected.
